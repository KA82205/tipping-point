How the model works

The State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model by Peter K. Enns, Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny predicts the Democratic share of the two-party vote in every state, then simulates the Electoral College 70,000 times.

Democratic two-party share in a state = 49.38 + 0.82 × state deviation from the national vote, previous election + 0.33 × state presidential approval (signed toward the Democrat) + 1.43 × weighted change in the state coincident economic index + 2.47 × presidential candidate's home state − 3.49 × presidential candidate's home state, previous election + 1.87 × running mate's home state − 0.35 × Anderson 1980 share (1984 only) − 0.60 × Perot 1996 share (2000 only)

Fitted on 11 elections × 51 jurisdictions, 1980–2020 (N = 561). Adjusted R² 0.90, standard error 3.46 points.

Uncertainty

Each forecast runs 10,000 draws of the coefficients, adds model error with a standard deviation equal to the model's root mean square error, and repeats across seven values of the economic weighting parameter: 70,000 simulations.

Maine and Nebraska districts take the statewide forecast plus each district's gap from its state in the previous election.

Ratings

Solid when a candidate wins more than 98 in 100 simulations, Likely above 75, Lean above 55, Toss-up otherwise.

The 2024 scorecard's state forecasts and 90% ranges are read from the paper's Figure A-2, which plots each state's simulated distribution; the recovered values reproduce every call and rating in the paper.

Data behind the 2028 tracker

State economies come from the Philadelphia Fed State Coincident Indexes via FRED. Trump's national approval is an average of public polls from the VoteHub Polling API. Until state-level estimates are available, each state's approval is the national average shifted by its 2024 Trump margin.

A few settings the paper does not publish, such as the approval baseline and the economic weighting parameter, use values consistent with it and will be checked against the replication data.

Sources

Enns, Colner, Kumar & Lagodny (2024), “Understanding Biden's Exit and the 2024 Election”, PS: Political Science & Politics, CC BY-NC 4.0. Replication data: Harvard Dataverse.
