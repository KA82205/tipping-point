About this site

Tipping Point is a personal, non-commercial project. Both models are published research in PS: Political Science & Politics: the State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model by Peter K. Enns (Cornell University and Verasight), Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny, and Jay A. DeSart's long-range state-level forecast. The models and their published forecasts are theirs. This site is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of the authors, and any errors in the code are its own.

The name comes from the state that decides the Electoral College, and the logo is that idea drawn as districts on a balance beam.

Data: FRED (Philadelphia Fed state coincident indexes), VoteHub and PollResults.org (poll data, CC BY 4.0), Civiqs (state approval, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0), Wikipedia, certified 2024 returns.

The Nowcast runs both models every morning on the latest polls and state economic data and averages them. It shows what the models say if the election were held today. It is not the forecast. The final forecast, due August 6, 2028, will average the forecasts that the two models' authors publish themselves.

The Nowcast is a close estimate of the two models, not an exact copy. It uses their authors' equations but not all of their data: the president's approval, nationally and in each state, comes from public polls here, where the authors make their own estimates. This does not affect the final forecast, which uses the forecasts the authors publish themselves. The hope is that the Nowcast sits close to their average by then.

How the forecast is coming together

    The final 2028 forecast will be the average of the two forecasts the authors themselves publish. DeSart's long-range forecast comes first, from head-to-head polls conducted in October 2027. Enns and colleagues' comes about 100 days before the election, from approval polls conducted in June and July 2028 and the economy through June. Until each is out, the site's own run of that model stands in: the long-range run is fixed on November 7, 2027 and the approval run on August 6, 2028, a week after each paper's cutoff so that polls published a few days late are included. The forecast is posted here on August 6, 2028 with whatever the authors have published by then, and is final once both of their forecasts are in. In the meantime, view the Nowcast to see what the election might look like if it were held today.

    How the models work

    The 2028 Nowcast runs two published models and averages them. Each predicts the Democratic share of the two-party vote in every state; the Electoral College is then simulated from their average. The Archive shows how that same average, and each model on its own, did in every election from 2008 to 2024.

    State-level approval and fundamentals model

    The State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model, by Peter K. Enns, Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = 49.38 + 0.82 × state deviation from the national vote, previous election + 0.33 × state presidential approval (signed toward the Democrat) + 1.43 × weighted change in the state coincident economic index + 2.47 × presidential candidate's home state − 3.49 × presidential candidate's home state, previous election + 1.87 × running mate's home state − 0.35 × Anderson 1980 share (1984 only) − 0.60 × Perot 1996 share (2000 only)

    Fitted on 11 elections × 51 jurisdictions, 1980–2020 (N = 561). Adjusted R² 0.90, standard error 3.46 points.

    The Nowcast runs this regression refitted on 1980–2024 from the authors' replication data, with the 2024 results added, as the authors refit before each forecast. Refitting on 1980–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added, and the approval baseline re-chosen the way the authors choose it: approval 0.30, economy 1.56, home state 2.31, previous home state −3.06, running mate 1.77, standard error 3.44.

    Long-range model

    The Long-Range State-Level Forecast Model, by Jay A. DeSart. It was built to forecast a year ahead, before the nominees are known.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = − 27.423 + 1.017 × the state's Democratic share in the previous election + 0.524 × Democratic share in national head-to-head polls + 2.544 × home state (this election's nominees, minus the previous election's) − 0.988 × consecutive terms the president's party has held the White House (counted as positive for Democrats, negative for Republicans)

    Fitted on seven elections × 50 states, 1996–2020 (N = 350). R² 0.90, standard error 3.08 points.

    The Nowcast runs it refitted on 1996–2024 from DeSart's replication data, with 2024 added. Refitting on 1996–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added: previous share 1.007, polls 0.522, home state 1.91, terms −1.00, standard error 2.93.

    How they are combined

    The two models read different things: how people rate the president and how state economies are doing, and what voters say when asked to choose between named candidates. Each state's Nowcast is the average of the two models' forecasts for that state, half each. The Electoral College is then simulated once from those averages, the way the state-level approval and fundamentals model simulates it.

    Equal weights, because the back-tests found no mix that does reliably better: the best one moved around depending on how the test was run. Research on election forecasting has long found that a plain average of forecasts from different methods beats relying on one, and is hard to improve on by fitting weights (Graefe and colleagues 2014, 2015). Nothing in the combination is fitted to past results: both models run with their authors' equations, and each gets half.

    Other mixes were tested, from all long-range to all approval and fundamentals in steps of five points, on both back-tests described under Track record. None is worth switching to. Mixes are written with the approval and fundamentals share first.

    • The gain is tiny. On state results the best mix in hindsight, 30/70, beats equal weights by 0.07 points over 2008 to 2024; over 1996 to 2024, equal weights are themselves the best mix. On the national vote the two tests point in opposite directions, 45/55 in the first and 70/30 in the second, where 70/30 also calls the 2000 winner wrong.
    • The best mix does not stay put. In the 1996 to 2024 test it runs from all approval and fundamentals in 1996 to all long-range in 2020. A lean toward approval and fundamentals did better in every election up to 2008, and a lean toward the long-range model in every one since. Weights fitted on the first four elections, 65/35, would have made the last four worse than equal weights: a state miss of 2.05 points against 1.96.
    • Fitted weights fail a fair test. Chosen from other elections and scored on one left out, a fitted mix did worse than equal weights in six of eight comparisons and better in two. The eight: both back-tests, chosen from all the other elections or only the earlier ones, judged on state results and on the national vote.

    The method was fixed on November 12, 2026, and will not be changed on the strength of these same back-tests again, so that 2028 is a fair test of it.

    A close estimate, not an exact copy. The Nowcast runs both models with their authors' equations, but it cannot use all of their data. The state economies come from the same source the authors use. Approval does not: the authors estimate it in each state from surveys that are not all public, so the Nowcast takes the national figure from public polls and the gaps between states from Civiqs's tracker. The head-to-head polls come from a different archive than DeSart's. So the Nowcast is a close estimate of what the two models say, not the authors' own numbers. Tested on 2020 and 2024, the approval model run on state figures of this kind missed the average state by no more than it did on the authors' own, though in 2020 it called 47 states to their 49 (table below). None of this reaches the final forecast, described next, which uses the forecasts the authors publish themselves. The hope is that the Nowcast sits close to their average by then. Every difference is listed under Everything that differs from the Enns model and Where the long-range model differs from DeSart's paper.

    The final forecast is the authors' own. The site's runs of the two models are for the Nowcast. The final forecast will be the average of the two forecasts the authors themselves publish for 2028, state by state. DeSart's comes first, about a year before the election: when it is out, his state forecasts replace the site's run of his model and stay fixed. Enns and colleagues publish about 100 days before the election: when theirs is out, it replaces the site's run of their model, and the forecast is final. Until each is out, the site's own run of that model stands in, fixed at its paper's cutoff (November 7, 2027 for the long-range model, August 6, 2028 for the approval model). If an author publishes no state forecasts before Election Day, that stand-in is used and marked as one. The reason is accuracy: the authors work from data the site can only approximate, above all approval measured state by state, and the forecasts they published did as well as the back-tests or better. This was set on November 20, 2026, before either 2028 forecast existed. Until then the plan was to freeze the site's own runs on August 6, 2028 and call that the forecast. If an author also revises the model itself, the site's stand-in run switches to the revised version and says so here.

    Three corrections since then. On November 20, 2026 the site was checked line by line against both papers, their appendices and the authors' own code, and three things did not follow the approval model's paper. They were changed to follow it. None came from a back-test or from any result.

    • Who is polled. The national approval average counted polls of registered and likely voters. The paper measures approval among all adults, so only polls of all adults count now.
    • The approval baseline. The authors re-choose it before each forecast as the value that best fits the earlier elections. The site had kept their 2024 value, 44. With 2024 added their rule gives 43.
    • The paper's own window. For polls conducted June 1 to July 30, 2028 the paper pools every survey answer. The site would have taken each pollster's latest poll and favored recent ones. It will now count every poll in that window by its sample size. This changes nothing before June 2028.

    On the data of November 19, the first lowered Trump's approval from 37.2% to 33.8% and raised the Democrat's chance from 50.6% to 56.8%; the second brought it to 55.1%. No state changed hands. The day-by-day history on this site starts again on November 20, so that it is one consistent series. See National approval and Approval baseline, under Everything that differs from the Enns model.

    A second check the same day, on how polls are read. Every poll rule was run again on the authors' own material. The rules held: the long-range model's poll average, worked out the site's way from DeSart's own lists of polls, gives the figures in his paper, and the approval model's refit gives the paper's table exactly. Four slips in how the site reads its two poll feeds were fixed.

    • One count per poll. The two feeds sometimes spell a pollster's name differently, such as Marist College and Marist University, and such a poll was counted twice. It counts once now. In the paper's window, two different polls by one pollster that end on the same day now both count.
    • Adults first. When one feed listed a poll only for registered voters and the other also carried its figures for all adults, the all-adults figures were dropped. They are kept now.
    • Like with like. The vice-president adjustment compares the vice president's rating with the president's in the same survey. It now also compares the same group of people: one survey had paired the vice president among registered voters with the president among all adults.
    • Centering the states. The state figures were centered on the national average with each state weighted by its 2024 votes. A national poll of all adults is the average of the states weighted by their adult populations, so that is the weighting now. Every state's approval rose by 0.14 points.

    On the data of November 20 these moved the Democrat's chance from 54.7% to 54.6%, and no state or rating changed. None of the first three touched a poll from the last 30 days; in earlier weeks the first two had moved the approval average by up to half a point.

    One setting was added the same day: how far apart the states sit. The approval model was fitted on its authors' own estimates of approval in each state. The Nowcast cannot make those estimates, so it takes the gaps between states from Civiqs, and Civiqs's gaps are wider. In the two elections where both exist, a state that Civiqs put 10 points from the country sat about 5 points from it in the authors' estimates: their gap was 0.56 times Civiqs's for Trump in 2020 and 0.35 times for Biden in 2024, or 0.48 times with the two together. Fed Civiqs's gaps in full, the model stretched the map in both tests below: Democratic states came out too Democratic and Republican states too Republican. So each state's gap now counts at half. This step is the site's own. The paper has no need of it, because the authors use their own estimates.

    The track record was corrected the same day. Its back-tests of the approval model had left out the Southern-states term that the authors' own back-tests keep through 2020. It was put back, which reproduces the accuracy the paper reports. The approval model's count for 2008 to 2024 went from 236 to 239 of 250 state results; the Nowcast's stayed at 240. This changes how past elections are scored, not how 2028 is forecast.

    One input was lined up with the authors' data on November 21, 2026. The District of Columbia has no economic index of its own. The authors average Maryland's and Virginia's indexes month by month and treat the result like a state's index. The site had averaged the two states' finished figures instead. The authors' replication file shows the difference: Virginia counts for 47% to 51% of the District's figure, its share of the two indexes, where averaging the finished figures gives it exactly half. The two ways differ in the fourth decimal of the District's figure, and no forecast, chance or rating on the site changed.

    2008 to 2024 are the elections both models can be tested on fairly: the long-range model's data starts in 1996, and with fewer than three past elections its equation cannot be estimated properly. The national figures add up the 50 states by turnout, leaving out the District of Columbia, so they differ slightly from the papers' own.     The Nowcast called four of the five winners; like both models on their own, it called 2016 wrong, giving Clinton 285 electoral votes. Its average miss in a state was 2.17 points, against 2.69 and 2.21 for the two models alone, and it called 240 of the 250 state results, against 239 and 238.

    Against a simple baseline. Safe states make any method look good, so the fair comparison is with assuming every state votes as it did four years earlier. That gets 222 of the 250 results (89%), against the Nowcast's 240 (96%). In the 73 results decided by under 10 points it gets 49 (67%), against 64 (88%). Of the 28 results where a state changed party, the Nowcast called 21. On the national vote, assuming a repeat of the last election misses by 2.3 points on average, against 1.0.

    Its national miss is smaller than either model's because their misses often point in opposite directions and partly cancel: in 2012 the state-level approval and fundamentals model was 1.9 points too low and the long-range model 1.7 too high, so the average was off by 0.1. When both miss the same way, as in 2020, the average does too.

    A second test, which forecasts each election from 1996 to 2024 using all the other elections, gives the Nowcast a state miss of 2.2 points and a national miss of 1.0, with seven of eight winners called.

    States moving together. The long-range model shifts every state by nearly the same amount from its last result. Three other ways of placing states were tried in both tests, scored on how far each state's position relative to the national vote was off, and none did better. The approval and fundamentals model's own map was worse in the first test (2.40 points against 2.00) and level in the second (2.10 against 2.15). Adding each state's result from two elections back to the long-range equation changed almost nothing. Starting from the average of each state's last two results was much worse. States have moved together more closely in 2020 and 2024 than in any election from 1996 to 2016, a trend Jackman (2014) found after 2012. In 2020 about two-thirds of the long-range model's error in the states was a miss shared by all of them: its national forecast was 54.8 against a result of 52.2, mostly because the October 2019 polls had the Democrat at 54.9.

    The long-range model is tested here on polls from October of the year before the election, as DeSart designed it. The Nowcast feeds it polls from even earlier, so today it is probably less accurate than this record shows.

    The Southern-states term

    What it is. Through 2020 the approval model had one more input: a marker for the eleven states of the old Confederacy. For decades those states voted more Republican than their last result, the president's approval and the economy would suggest, and the marker let the model allow for that.

    Why the authors dropped it. The pattern faded. It was clear in the 1980s and faint in the 1990s and early 2000s. In recent elections the authors find no sign of it once a state's past vote is taken into account, which they put down to population change in the South. A marker that no longer adds anything only makes a model more complicated, so their 2024 forecast leaves it out (Enns and colleagues 2024, and their online appendix 3). The Nowcast runs that 2024 version, so it has no Southern-states term either.

    What the back-tests do with it. The authors' own code keeps the term in every before-the-fact forecast through 2020, because that is how the model was run at the time. The track record above does the same. Run that way, the approval model's back-tests reproduce the accuracy the paper reports: 91% of states called from 1984 to 2020, 95% from 2004, and only Georgia missed in 2020.

    Some third models look a little better than none in the first table: the objective keys and Time for Change all lower the state miss by about 0.2 points and the national miss by 0.1 to 0.2. That is not enough to act on, for three reasons.

    • The whole gain is one election. In 2020 both models overshot the Democrat by about 3 points, and anything that pulled the other way helped. Leave 2020 out (second table) and no third model beats none on the national vote.
    • Choosing by the back-test does not survive a fair test. For each of the five elections, the third model with the best national record in the other four was picked and then scored on the one left out. The pick changed almost every time (Time for Change, six keys, the Primary Model, none, six keys) and the result was a national miss of 1.46 points, worse than the 0.98 from using no third model at all.
    • The longer test does not back a third model either. Forecasting each election from 1996 to 2024 from all the others, adding the keys leaves the national miss unchanged at 1.0 and calls one fewer winner.

    So the Nowcast uses the two published models and nothing else.

    Guarding against fitting the past too closely

    A method can be made to look good on past elections by trying many versions and keeping the best. With five elections to test on, that is a real risk, and an earlier version of this Nowcast ran it: it added a third model, a count of Lichtman's keys, after comparing eight candidates on the same five elections. The check above showed that kind of choosing does not hold up, so it was undone.

    What is left involves no choosing. The two state models are published models run with their authors' equations; nothing in them was tuned here. They are averaged with equal weights, the default when there is no strong reason for anything else. The record quoted on this site is that plain average's record. Each back-test forecast uses only the elections before it. And the method is now fixed: it was frozen on November 12, 2026, before any 2028 result could inform it. Three corrections were made on November 20, 2026, each to follow the approval model's paper more closely, and four slips in reading the poll feeds were fixed the same day. One setting was added that day from a measurement made here, not from a paper: the gaps between states in the Civiqs figures count at half. On November 21 the District of Columbia's economy figure was lined up with the authors' data, which moved nothing. All are listed with their effect under How they are combined.

    The evidence is still thinner than it looks. There are 250 state results, but states move together in any one year, so they amount to about five independent tests. A 1.0-point national miss and 96% of states is what the method did on those five; 2020 shows it can miss by 3 points when both models err the same way.

    Primary Model. Norpoth's forecasts as he published them: 50.1% for Obama in 2008, 53.2% for Obama in 2012 and 47.5% for Clinton in 2016, then electoral votes only, 362 for Trump in 2020 and 315 for the Democrat in 2024. The last two were turned into the national vote that would produce them on the previous map. On its own the model missed the national vote by 3.8 points on average and the electoral vote by 64, against 1.0 and 22 for the Nowcast's average. It cannot run until the first primaries in early 2028, so it was left out.

    Time for Change. Abramowitz's model predicts the national vote from the president's June net approval, second-quarter economic growth and whether the president's party is seeking a second term. Refitted from public data (Gallup's June approval and the government's growth figures, as compiled for Harvard's Gov 1347 forecasting course), it reproduces his 2024 forecast to within 0.3 points. It needs a judgment call whenever the economy has a shock like the 2020 lockdown quarter, which is handled here as he handled it, without the growth term. As a third model it lowers the state miss from 2.17 points to 1.96 and the national miss from 0.98 to 0.91 over 2008 to 2024, but on the national vote it does worse than no third model without 2020. It reads mostly the same things as the state-level approval and fundamentals model, and it cannot run until late July 2028, so it was left out.

    The Nowcast page shows each model on its own next to the average, simulated the same way.

    Uncertainty

    As in the authors' code: each forecast draws the coefficients 10,000 times, together, from their covariance; adds model error with a standard deviation equal to the model's root mean square error; and repeats for seven values of the economic weighting, 0.52 to 0.58. That makes 70,000 simulations. The Nowcast uses the same simulation for the averaged forecast.

    What that means for the average. Neither paper simulates an average of two models, so this part is the site's own adaptation of the authors' simulation, not something the authors designed or tested. In each simulation the coefficients drawn are the approval model's; the long-range model's forecast is held at its point value; and the model error added is the approval model's in full. The average has missed by less than that model alone, so the uncertainty shown is on the wide side: it is not fitted to the average's own record.

    When an author's own forecast is put in. Once DeSart or Enns and colleagues publish their 2028 forecast, its state forecasts replace the site's run of that model. That half is then held at its published value in every simulation, as the long-range half always is, and the model error is added as before. When both halves are the authors' own, nothing is drawn but that error. The District of Columbia, and any state an author gives no number for, keeps the site's own figure. Run this way on the two forecasts the authors published for 2024, the simulation gives the Democrat 31 in 100, the figure on the Archive's 2024 page, and 27 in 100 for Enns and colleagues' forecast alone, as they published. The numbers are read from the authors' table, data file or chart by hand and put in with pipeline/put_in.py.

    How it has held up. Applying the same error to each back-test forecast of 2008 to 2024 (without the coefficient draws, which the live simulation adds and which make its ranges slightly wider still), the stated 50% range held 68% of the 250 state results, the 80% range 90% and the 90% range 94%; the simulation assumed misses of 3.5 points per state where the average's were 3.0. States rated Lean (66 in 100 on average) were won 77% of the time, Likely (90 in 100) 96%, and all 137 Solid states were won. The Democrat's chances would have been 91, 72, 61, 84 and 30 in 100 in 2008 to 2024, and the Democrat won the first, second and fourth. So the ranges have been wider than needed, and the odds more cautious than the results: that shows the simulation has not been overconfident, not that it is well calibrated. Five elections cannot settle that, and narrowing the ranges to fit them would be fitting the past, so the simulation keeps the authors' error model unchanged.

    The errors of different states are correlated, so when the model misses, it tends to miss the whole map in the same direction. The authors draw that correlation once for each weighting, between 0.70 and 0.97, and so does this site. Rerunning their code on their replication data gives Harris 27 in 100 in 2024, as published, and this site's engine reproduces it.

    The long-range model's own paper gives each state an independent error and shares none. That makes a national miss nearly impossible: its 95% range for the 2024 national vote was 49.4% to 51.9% Democratic, and the result, 49.3%, fell outside it. Its own record shows national misses of 1 to 2 points, which move every state together. So the Nowcast simulates both models with the correlated error above. This site's version of DeSart's equation, run with his independent errors on his 2024 inputs from his replication files, reproduces his published simulation (256 Democratic electoral votes on average, 95% between 218 and 306), which is how it was checked.

    Maine and Nebraska districts take the statewide forecast plus each district's gap from its state in the previous election, as in the authors' code.

    Ratings

    Solid when a candidate wins more than 98 in 100 simulations, Likely above 75, Lean above 55, Toss-up otherwise.

    Everything that differs from the Enns model

    The Archive's 2024 page shows the Enns model's own forecast. The 2028 Nowcast runs the same model, refitted with 2024 from the authors' replication files. These are all the places where it does something the authors did not, or fills in something their files do not give. Each is a setting in pipeline/config.json or a line in the site's code.

    Changed or added on purpose

    One more model is averaged in. Each state's forecast is half the Enns model and half DeSart's long-range model, then simulated together. The paper uses its model alone. Why: see "How they are combined" above.

    Each party's field is weighted until its nominee is known. The paper forecasts once the nominees are set, so it has no rule for this. Home-state effects are spread across the possible nominees, an idea taken from DeSart, but by each candidate's share of primary polling. DeSart's own model, as run here, spreads them by how often each candidate appears in the head-to-head polls. The vice-president adjustment is applied in the same proportion: if the sitting vice president has 38% of his party's primary polling, 38% of the adjustment applies.

    The vice-president adjustment is phased in until June 2028. The authors apply the gap between the vice president's and the president's net approval in full, from a handful of July polls that asked about both. The site measures it the same way: in surveys that asked about both, comparing the same group of people. In the daily Nowcast it is scaled by the number of polls of the vice president and reaches full size at 20 polls, so a few polls cannot move every state at once. Once the approval model is on the paper's window, June 1 to July 30, 2028, the gap is applied in full, as the authors apply it, so the final forecast uses the full adjustment. When there are too few polls in the last 120 days, the window widens to 240 and then 365 days, and as a last resort a single poll is used.

    It runs every day. The paper forecasts once, about 100 days before the election, from approval polls taken in June and July and the economy through June. The Nowcast uses today's polls and the latest economic month, so it describes today, not November 2028. The site's last run follows the paper's timing: approval polls conducted June 1 to July 30, 2028 and the economy through June. It stands in for this model's half of the final forecast until Enns and colleagues publish their own. The Nowcast moves to that window in June 2028, once ten pollsters have a poll in it; until then it keeps the last 30 days, so the first days of June do not rest on one or two polls.

    National vote. The authors add up the national vote by weighting each state by its 2020 population. The Nowcast weights states by the votes cast in 2024, which is how DeSart adds up his. On the Nowcast of November 20, 2026 the two ways gave the same figure to within 0.01 points.

    From other sources, or inferred

    State approval. The authors estimate approval in each state themselves from national surveys, some of which cannot be shared. The Nowcast uses Civiqs's daily tracker of Trump's job approval instead. Civiqs reports the share of registered voters who approve in each state. Only its differences between states are kept, not its level, and they count at half: each state is set to today's national average plus half that state's gap from the Civiqs average. That average weights each state by its adult population, since a national poll of all adults is the same kind of average. The gaps count at half because the authors' estimates, which the model was fitted on, sit about half as far from the national figure as Civiqs's do: 0.48 times, measured on 2020 and 2024. The test is under How they are combined. The District of Columbia is not in that source and is assumed to sit 24 points below the average state, about where the authors' own estimate had it in 2020, so its gap is not halved. The figures are a copy taken on November 19, 2026. Civiqs's site does not accept automatic reads, so the copy is refreshed by hand: the page's source is opened in a browser and saved, and Civiqs's own figures are read from it, to two decimals. Until November 19, 2026 the page as shown was saved instead, which gave whole percentages. Civiqs has been asked to allow a daily automatic read. From the copy of November 18 to the copy of November 19, the first with decimals, a typical state moved 0.7 points against the average and the largest move was 2.8 points, in Montana. In the Nowcast that came to 0.1 points for a typical state and 0.4 at most, with the gaps still counted in full.

    What the state figures change. As a check, the Nowcast's current rules were run three ways on the data of November 20, 2026: with Civiqs's gaps between states in full, at half as used, and with no gaps at all, every state at the national average. The chance of a Democratic win was 54.5%, 56.1% and 57.6%. The electoral vote count, the tipping-point state and the winner of every state were the same in all three. Between full and half, the approval figure moves 3.9 points in the average state and 9.6 at most, in North Dakota; in the Nowcast the average state's share moves 0.6 points and North Dakota's 1.4. Three ratings differ between full and half (Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Kansas) and four more between half and none (Colorado, Illinois, Missouri and South Carolina). A fourth run, with the gaps taken from each state's 2024 vote and not from Civiqs, gave 57.1%.

    Florida and the last nominee's home state. The model gives a nominee extra votes in their home state and takes them back at the next election if they are not running again. The authors count Florida as Trump's home state, and he cannot run in 2028, so the model moves Florida toward the Democrat. In the paper's fit that move is 3.49 points, which looked too large, and an earlier version of this site cut it in half by hand. It no longer does. The model is now refitted with the 2024 results, as the authors refit before each forecast, and the 2024 evidence itself lowers the move to 3.06 points; halving it as well would count that evidence twice. California, Harris's home state, moves 3.06 points the other way. The long-range model has its own, smaller version of the same effect: 1.91 points.

    National approval. An average of public polls of all adults from VoteHub and PollResults.org over the last 30 days: each pollster's latest poll, weighted by sample size and by age, with recent polls counting more. Internal and partisan polls are left out. A poll that both sources list counts once, however they spell the pollster's name, and a poll that reports all adults and voters separately counts by its all-adults figures. Polls of registered or likely voters only are left out too, because the paper measures approval among all adults: its estimates cover everyone aged 18 and over, are weighted to census population counts, and come from surveys of the general public (Gallup, AP-NORC, Verasight and YouGov in 2024). In every month since May 2026, polls of voters have read 3 to 6 points higher for Trump than polls of all adults. On the paper's window, June 1 to July 30, 2028, every poll of all adults in the window counts, by its sample size alone and with no extra weight for newer polls, because the paper pools every survey answer from June and July.

    Approval baseline: 43. The model reads approval minus a baseline. The authors re-choose the baseline before each forecast: the value that best fits the elections before it. Their replication file stores approval with the baseline already taken off, without stating it: 45 for the forecasts of 2000 to 2020 and 44 for 2024, and their rule applied to their data reproduces each of those. With the 2024 results added the same rule gives 43, so the Nowcast uses 43. The site used 44, the 2024 value, until November 20, 2026, and 50 before the replication files arrived.

    Taken from the authors' replication files

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting the authors' data with the 2024 results added. This lowers the fade in the previous nominees' home states from 3.49 to 3.06 points. Until now the site halved the fade, because it looked too large in 2024; the refit now learns that from the 2024 results, so halving it as well would count the same evidence twice. The authors code Trump's 2020 run as Florida; DeSart codes it as New York.

    Economic weighting. 0.55, the median fitted value for 2008 to 2016, simulated from 0.52 to 0.58 in steps of 0.01, as in the authors' forecast code. The table in their paper, shown at the top of this page, is fitted at 0.56; refitted with 2024 at that value the economy coefficient is 1.59, not 1.56. The site used 0.40 to 0.70. Economic growth is counted against the Democrat while a Republican is president, as the site assumed.

    Uncertainty. Coefficients are drawn together from their covariance, and the correlation of state errors is drawn between 0.70 and 0.97 for each weighting. The site drew coefficients one at a time and used a fixed split of the error.

    District of Columbia economy and Maine and Nebraska districts. DC's economy is Maryland's and Virginia's indexes averaged month by month, and each district follows its state's swing. Until November 21, 2026 the site averaged the two states' finished figures for DC instead, which differs in the fourth decimal.

    Where the long-range model differs from DeSart's paper

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting his replication data with 2024 added: his October 2023 poll average (49.53%), the actual nominees' home states and the result. That lowers the home-state effect from 2.54 to 1.91 points.

    Timing. DeSart forecasts once, from polls taken in October of the year before the election. The site's run does the same: polls conducted in October 2027, fixed on November 7, 2027, with home states spread by poll appearances whoever the nominees turn out to be. Until those polls are fixed, the Nowcast uses the last 180 days of polls. When DeSart publishes his own forecast from the October 2027 polls, his state forecasts replace the site's run and are what the final forecast uses.

    Error. It is simulated with the correlated error described above, not his independent state errors.

    Which polls. As in the paper, every head-to-head matchup between two named possible nominees counts once in a plain average, and generic party ballots and three-way questions are left out. So are pairings with a two-term president, who cannot be a nominee. Matchups with people outside the site's candidate list count too, and their home states share in the home-state effect. One difference: DeSart took his polls from the RealClearPolitics archive, while the site reads PollResults.org.

    Districts. DeSart awards Maine and Nebraska statewide. Here their districts follow the rule above.
