Which way is 2028 tipping?

A daily Nowcast of the presidential race, built as an ensemble of three models: the State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model by Peter K. Enns and colleagues, Jay DeSart's long-range polls model, and five of Allan Lichtman's objective Keys to the White House. One reads how voters rate the president and how state economies are doing, one reads early head-to-head polls, and one reads the political and economic record of the party in power. The Nowcast is their average.

About this site

Tipping Point is a personal, non-commercial project. It runs the State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model created by Peter K. Enns (Cornell University and Verasight), Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny, published in PS: Political Science & Politics. The model and its published forecasts are theirs. The 2028 Nowcast also runs a second published model from the same journal, Jay A. DeSart's long-range state-level forecast, which reads head-to-head polls, and a third built from five of Allan Lichtman's Keys to the White House. This site is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of the authors, and any errors in the code are its own.

The idea: presidential elections are largely decided by fundamentals that are knowable months ahead. The authors publish their forecast about 100 days before Election Day. The name comes from the state that decides the Electoral College, and the logo is that idea drawn as districts on a balance beam.

The 2024 scorecard checks the Nowcast's method against the certified 2024 results, state by state: a back-test that averages the two forecasts published before that election and lets the keys set a third of the national level. The approval and economy forecast, as its authors published it in July 2024, can be scored on its own there too. Both published forecasts are recomputed from their authors' replication files.

The 2028 Nowcast runs that model every morning on the latest Trump approval polls and state economic data, runs DeSart's model on the latest head-to-head polls, scores the keys, and averages the three. Until the nominees are confirmed, each party's possible nominees are averaged by their standing in the polls. It shows what the models say if the election were held today. It is not the forecast, which needs the nominees and June–July 2028 surveys and is due July 30, 2028.

Everything updates automatically: polls, state economies, and the nominees, running mates and home states once the conventions pick them. Data: FRED (Philadelphia Fed state coincident indexes), VoteHub and PollResults.org (poll data, CC BY 4.0), Wikipedia, certified 2024 returns.

    How the models work

    The 2024 scorecard grades one published model. The 2028 Nowcast runs that model and two more, and averages the three. Each predicts the Democratic share of the two-party vote in every state; the Electoral College is then simulated from their average.

    Approval and economy

    The State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model, by Peter K. Enns, Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = 49.38 + 0.82 × state deviation from the national vote, previous election + 0.33 × state presidential approval (signed toward the Democrat) + 1.43 × weighted change in the state coincident economic index + 2.47 × presidential candidate's home state − 3.49 × presidential candidate's home state, previous election + 1.87 × running mate's home state − 0.35 × Anderson 1980 share (1984 only) − 0.60 × Perot 1996 share (2000 only)

    Fitted on 11 elections × 51 jurisdictions, 1980–2020 (N = 561). Adjusted R² 0.90, standard error 3.46 points.

    The Nowcast runs this regression refitted on 1980–2024 from the authors' replication data, with the 2024 results added, as the authors refit before each forecast. Refitting on 1980–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added: approval 0.30, economy 1.56, home state 2.31, previous home state −3.07, running mate 1.78, standard error 3.45.

    Head-to-head polls

    The Long-Range State-Level Forecast Model, by Jay A. DeSart. It was built to forecast a year ahead, before the nominees are known.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = − 27.423 + 1.017 × the state's Democratic share in the previous election + 0.524 × Democratic share in national head-to-head polls + 2.544 × home state (this election's nominees, minus the previous election's) − 0.988 × consecutive terms the president's party has held the White House (counted as positive for Democrats, negative for Republicans)

    Fitted on seven elections × 50 states, 1996–2020 (N = 350). R² 0.90, standard error 3.08 points.

    The Nowcast runs it refitted on 1996–2024 from DeSart's replication data, with 2024 added. Refitting on 1996–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added: previous share 1.007, polls 0.522, home state 1.91, terms −1.00, standard error 2.93.

    Five objective keys

    Allan Lichtman's Keys to the White House are thirteen true-or-false statements about the party holding the White House. Seven of them, such as scandal and charisma, are his judgment calls. Six can be scored from the record. The Nowcast uses five of them and counts how many are false. The sixth, no significant third-party campaign, is clear after an election (a candidate with 5% of the vote) but before one it rests on polls of candidates who may never run, and the tests cannot tell five keys from six (see Why five keys and not six). Lichtman forecasts only who wins, never states, and neither does this model: it gives one national vote share, which sets a third of the Nowcast's national level.

    Two-party share of the party in the White House = 57.83 − 2.96 × number of the five keys that are false 1 Party mandate: more House seats after the midterms than after the previous midterms 2 No primary contest: the nominee wins two-thirds of first-ballot delegates, without deep party divisions 3 The party's candidate is the sitting president 5 No recession, real or widely believed, during the campaign 6 Real growth per person this term at least the average of the previous two terms

    Fitted on 41 elections, 1864–2024, with Lichtman's own ratings as tabulated on Wikipedia. Standard error 4.94 points.

    The share is for the party holding the White House, whichever party that is. In 2028 it is the Republicans, so the Democrat's share is 100 minus it. With 2 keys false the equation gives the Republican 51.9% and the Democrat 48.1%. With 3 false it gives the Republican 49.0% and the Democrat 51.0%. Today's count is 2.5, which puts the Democrat at 49.6%. Each key is worth about 3 points of the national vote in this model. In the Nowcast the keys carry a third of the national level, so one key turning false moves every state about 1 point toward the Democrat, by the same amount in every state.

    The count sorts elections well at the ends. In the middle it is far less sure: with 2 keys false the party in power won the national vote 5 times in 9, with 3 it won once in 8, and 2028 stands between the two. It explains about two-fifths of the differences in the vote from one election to the next. Forecasting each of the 41 elections from the other 40, it misses the national vote by 3.6 points on average (2.7 since 1948) and picks the national-vote winner 36 times. That is a larger miss than the other two models make, which is why the keys get only a third of the national level and no say in which state is ahead of which.

    Each key is scored by Lichtman's own rule, and each updates as soon as its evidence does:

    • 1, party mandate: false. Democrats won the House in November 2026, so Republicans hold fewer seats than the 222 they won in 2022. Fixed until the next midterm.
    • 2, no primary contest: Lichtman's rule is that the nominee wins at least two-thirds of the delegates on the first ballot at the convention, with no deep and vocal party divisions. That cannot be known until 2028, so it counts as half false: in the 16 elections since 1864 with no sitting president running, it was false in 8. The note beside it updates each morning with the Republican leader's share in national primary polls.
    • 3, sitting president running: false. Trump cannot run for a third term.
    • 5, short-term economy: scored each morning from FRED. Lichtman counts a recession that is officially under way or that the public believes is happening. The official dating comes months late, so the real-time Sahm rule stands in for it: the key is false if that indicator reaches 0.5 or real GDP per person falls two quarters in a row. A recession the public clearly believes in, as in 1992, is set by hand.
    • 6, long-term economy: scored each morning from FRED, by Lichtman's rule: yearly growth in real GDP per person this term against the average of the previous two terms.

    The model gives one national number and no states. Its odds on the Nowcast page are of winning the national vote, from that number and the model's standard error.

    How they are combined

    The three models read different things: how people rate the president and how state economies are doing; what voters say when asked to choose between named candidates; and the political and economic record of the party in power. Two of them forecast states. The keys forecast only a national vote, as Lichtman's keys always have. So the state map is the average of the approval and economy model and the polls model, half each, and the national level is the average of all three, a third each: every state moves by the same amount, a third of the gap between the keys' national vote and the map's. The Electoral College is then simulated once from that map, the way the approval and economy model simulates it.

    Equal weights, because the back-tests below found no mix that does reliably better. Every mix that gave each model between about a quarter and two-fifths of the weight did about equally well, and the best one moved around depending on how the test was run. Research on election forecasting has long found that averaging forecasts from different methods beats relying on one, and these back-tests agree.

    Misses are average misses in points of Democratic vote share. Counting the objective keys beat every way of weighting them. Weighting each key by its own past record did worse: each key has flipped only a handful of times since 1864, so weights fitted to history mostly fit its accidents. All 13 keys did worse than five or six, because the seven judgment keys add noise. Weights taken from a published machine-learning ranking of the keys (Virtual Tout's relative importance, which puts the primary contest and the short-term economy far ahead of the rest) did worse as well, and that ranking was learned from the same elections it would be tested on, so in a fair test it would do worse still. A ranking like that measures what each key adds once the others are known, so a rarely false but decisive key such as the primary contest ranks high; a count of how often each key matched the winner ranks them differently. Neither beats counting. Most of the gain from any third model comes from 2020, when both of the first two models overshot the Democrat by about 3 points nationally; without 2020 the first two models alone have the smallest national miss.

    Why five keys and not six

    Six of Lichtman's keys can be scored from the record, and in the table above six looks better than five: a national miss of 0.78 points against 0.90. The Nowcast still uses five, for three reasons.

    • The edge does not come from the third-party key. That key was true in all five elections from 2008 to 2024, so it never changed a count. Six did better only because fitting the equation with it gives the party in power about 0.6 points more, which happened to suit those five years.
    • Every longer test has five level or slightly ahead. The table below scores the keys on their own over far more elections.
    • Before an election the key cannot be scored the way the record scores it. In the record it is false when a third candidate won 5% of the vote. In advance, Lichtman's rule is a candidate consistently polling at 10%, and until someone does the key counts as true. Scored that way through history, six does no better than five (last column).

    Is this fitted too closely to the past?

    Partly, and the numbers above should be read with that in mind. The back-tests were also used to choose the method: whether to add a third model, which one, and five keys or six. Picking the best of several options on five elections flatters the result, so a 0.9-point national miss and 96% of states are probably a little better than the Nowcast will do in 2028.

    Four things limit the damage. The two state models are published models run with their authors' equations; nothing in them was tuned here. The weights are equal, not fitted, and the keys are counted, not weighted; every attempt to fit weights did worse. Each back-test forecast uses only the elections before it. And the options differ by a tenth of a point or two, less than five elections can resolve, so no choice among them was treated as proven.

    The evidence is also thinner than it looks. There are 250 state results, but states move together in any one year, so they amount to about five independent tests. What the tests do support is the broad point: an average of different models beats each model alone. The first two models together missed the national vote by 1.0 point, against 1.7 and 1.5 on their own.

    Primary Model. Norpoth's forecasts as he published them: 50.1% for Obama in 2008, 53.2% for Obama in 2012 and 47.5% for Clinton in 2016, then electoral votes only, 362 for Trump in 2020 and 315 for the Democrat in 2024. The last two were turned into the national vote that would produce them on the previous map. On its own the model missed the national vote by 3.8 points on average and the electoral vote by 64, against 1.0 and 22 for the Nowcast's average. It cannot run until the first primaries in early 2028, so it was left out.

    Time for Change. Abramowitz's model predicts the national vote from the president's June net approval, second-quarter economic growth and whether the president's party is seeking a second term. Refitted from public data (Gallup's June approval and the government's growth figures, as compiled for Harvard's Gov 1347 forecasting course), it reproduces his 2024 forecast to within 0.3 points. It needs a judgment call whenever the economy has a shock like the 2020 lockdown quarter, which is handled here as he handled it, without the growth term. Tested like for like it matches the five keys from 2008 to 2024 (1.89 points against 1.90 on states, 0.91 against 0.90 nationally) and does worse without 2020. It reads mostly the same things as the approval and economy model, and it cannot run until late July 2028, so it was left out.

    Uncertainty

    As in the authors' code: each forecast draws the coefficients 10,000 times, together, from their covariance; adds model error with a standard deviation equal to the model's root mean square error; and repeats for seven values of the economic weighting, 0.52 to 0.58. That makes 70,000 simulations. The Nowcast uses the same simulation for the averaged forecast.

    The errors of different states are correlated, so when the model misses, it tends to miss the whole map in the same direction. The authors draw that correlation once for each weighting, between 0.70 and 0.97, and so does this site. Rerunning their code on their replication data gives Harris 27 in 100 in 2024, as published, and this site's engine reproduces it.

    The polls model's own paper gives each state an independent error and shares none. That makes a national miss nearly impossible: its 95% range for the 2024 national vote was 49.4% to 51.9% Democratic, and the result, 49.3%, fell outside it. Its own record shows national misses of 1 to 2 points, which move every state together. So the Nowcast simulates both models with the correlated error above. This site's version of DeSart's equation, run with his independent errors on his 2024 inputs from his replication files, reproduces his published simulation (256 Democratic electoral votes on average, 95% between 218 and 306), which is how it was checked.

    Maine and Nebraska districts take the statewide forecast plus each district's gap from its state in the previous election, as in the authors' code.

    Data behind the Nowcast

It will be replaced by Civiqs or Morning Consult estimates if a data-sharing agreement is reached.

    The keys: real GDP per person (FRED series A939RX0Q048SBEA) and the real-time Sahm rule recession indicator (SAHMREALTIME), read each morning; the 2026 House result from Associated Press calls.

    The 2024 back-test on the scorecard: DeSart's published state forecasts for 2024, from the file dated November 5, 2023 in his replication data (doi:10.7910/DVN/HZGLY9), averaged with the approval and economy forecast published in July 2024.

    Nominees, running mates and their home states are read from Wikipedia each morning. The vice-president adjustment uses job-approval polls of the vice president from VoteHub, or favorability when there are none, as the authors did for 1988. The list of possible candidates follows national 2028 primary polls from PollResults.org.

    Head-to-head polls are national general-election polls between two people on the candidate list, over the last 180 days. They come from the PollResults.org feed behind its 2028 presidential polls page, which carries the New York Times poll data, and every record in it is read each morning. A poll that tests several pairings counts once for each. Left out: state polls, generic party ballots, questions that name a third candidate, and pairings with anyone not on the candidate list. VoteHub supplies the approval polls; it does not publish general-election pairings yet.

    Everything that differs from the Enns model

    The 2024 scorecard is the Enns model's own forecast. The 2028 Nowcast runs the same model, refitted with 2024 from the authors' replication files. These are all the places where it does something the authors did not, or fills in something their files do not give. Each is a setting in pipeline/config.json or a line in the site's code.

    Changed or added on purpose

    Two more models are averaged in. The state map is half the Enns model and half DeSart's head-to-head polls model. The national level is the average of those two and five of Lichtman's objective keys, which forecast no states. The map is then simulated together. The paper uses its model alone. Why: see "How they are combined" above.

    Each party's field is weighted until its nominee is known. The paper forecasts once the nominees are set, so it has no rule for this. Following DeSart, home-state effects are spread across the possible nominees by their share of primary polling. The vice-president adjustment is applied in the same proportion: if the sitting vice president has 38% of his party's primary polling, 38% of the adjustment applies.

    The vice-president adjustment is phased in. The authors apply the gap between the vice president's and the president's net approval in full, from a handful of July polls. Here it is scaled by the number of polls of the vice president and reaches full size at 20 polls, so a few polls cannot move every state at once. When there are too few polls in the last 120 days, the window widens to 240 and then 365 days, and as a last resort a single poll is used.

    It runs every day. The paper forecasts once, about 100 days before the election, from approval polls taken in June and July and the economy through June. The Nowcast uses today's polls and the latest economic month, so it describes today, not November 2028. The official forecast on the countdown will follow the paper's timing.

    National vote. The authors add up the national vote by weighting each state by its 2020 population. The Nowcast weights states by the votes cast in 2024.

    From other sources, or inferred

    State approval. The authors estimate approval in each state themselves from national surveys, some of which cannot be shared. The Nowcast uses G. Elliott Morris's state estimates of September 22, 2026, shifted so they agree with today's national average. The District of Columbia is not in that source and is set to 15%.

    National approval. An average of public polls from VoteHub and PollResults.org over the last 30 days: each pollster's latest poll, weighted by sample size and by age, with recent polls counting more. Internal and partisan polls are left out.

    Approval baseline: 44. The model reads approval minus a baseline. The replication data stores approval with the baseline already taken off, without stating it, but it is 44. The authors' surveys average 44.2% approval, the stored values shift by whole points from one forecast year to the next, and comparing them with national approval in each election from 1980 to 2024 gives 43.6, give or take 0.6. The site used 50 before these files arrived, which overstated the Democrat's share in the approval and economy model by about 2 points.

    Settled by the authors' replication files

    These were guesses until the files arrived. Each now follows the authors' data and code.

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting the authors' data with the 2024 results added. This lowers the fade in the previous nominees' home states from 3.49 to 3.07 points. Until now the site halved the fade, because it looked too large in 2024; the refit now learns that from the 2024 results, so halving it as well would count the same evidence twice. The authors code Trump's 2020 run as Florida; DeSart codes it as New York.

    Economic weighting. 0.55, the median fitted value for 2008 to 2016, simulated from 0.52 to 0.58 in steps of 0.01. The site used 0.40 to 0.70. Economic growth is counted against the Democrat while a Republican is president, as the site assumed.

    Uncertainty. Coefficients are drawn together from their covariance, and the correlation of state errors is drawn between 0.70 and 0.97 for each weighting. The site drew coefficients one at a time and used a fixed split of the error.

    District of Columbia economy and Maine and Nebraska districts. DC uses the average of Maryland and Virginia, and each district follows its state's swing, as the site assumed.

    Where the polls model differs from DeSart's paper

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting his replication data with 2024 added: his October 2023 poll average (49.53%), the actual nominees' home states and the result. That lowers the home-state effect from 2.54 to 1.91 points.

    Timing. DeSart forecasts once, from polls taken in October of the year before the election, which will be October 2027. The Nowcast uses the last 180 days of polls.

    Error. It is simulated with the correlated error described above, not his independent state errors.

    Chosen nominees. If you pick a nominee with at least five head-to-head polls, those polls are used, as DeSart did for each pairing in 2016 and 2020. With fewer, a wider set is used and the page says so.

    Which polls. Pairings that name a third candidate, a generic party label or someone not on the candidate list are left out.

    Districts. DeSart awards Maine and Nebraska statewide. Here their districts follow the rule above.
