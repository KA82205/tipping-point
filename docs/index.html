The tipping point

The balance above lines every state up from most Democratic to most Republican, sized by electoral votes. The state that carries a candidate past 270 is the tipping point. Here is the same line-up for the 2028 Nowcast today, and for 2024, as forecast in July and as it turned out.

About this site

Tipping Point is a personal, non-commercial project. It runs the State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model created by Peter K. Enns (Cornell University and Verasight), Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny, published in PS: Political Science & Politics. The model and its published forecasts are theirs. The 2028 Nowcast also runs a second published model from the same journal, Jay A. DeSart's long-range state-level forecast, which reads head-to-head polls. This site is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of the authors, and any errors in the code are its own.

The idea: presidential elections are largely decided by fundamentals that are knowable months ahead. The authors publish their forecast about 100 days before Election Day. The name comes from the state that decides the Electoral College, and the logo is that idea drawn as districts on a balance beam.

The 2024 scorecard checks the Nowcast's method against the certified 2024 results, state by state: a back-test that averages the two forecasts published before that election. The two published forecasts can be scored on their own there too: the state-level approval and fundamentals model as its authors published it in July 2024, and DeSart's long-range model as he published it in November 2023, with his odds reproduced from his own simulation script. Both published forecasts are recomputed from their authors' replication files.

The 2028 Nowcast runs that model every morning on the latest Trump approval polls and state economic data, runs DeSart's model on the latest head-to-head polls, and averages the two. Until the nominees are confirmed, each party's possible nominees are averaged by their standing in the polls. It shows what the models say if the election were held today. It is not the forecast, which publishes August 6, 2028.

Everything updates automatically: polls, state economies, and the nominees, running mates and home states once the conventions pick them. Data: FRED (Philadelphia Fed state coincident indexes), VoteHub and PollResults.org (poll data, CC BY 4.0), Wikipedia, certified 2024 returns.

How the forecast is coming together

    The 2028 forecast follows each paper's own cutoff, by the date polls were conducted. The state-level approval and fundamentals model uses approval polls conducted June 1 to July 30, 2028 (100 days before the election) and the economy through June. The long-range model uses head-to-head polls conducted in October 2027, as DeSart does, and is fixed a week later, on November 7, 2027. The forecast publishes August 6, 2028, a week after the last cutoff, so polls that come out a few days late are included. Once published, it does not change: it is the Nowcast as it stood that day, frozen. In the meantime, view the Nowcast to see what the election might look like if it were held today.

    How the models work

    The 2028 Nowcast runs two published models and averages them. Each predicts the Democratic share of the two-party vote in every state; the Electoral College is then simulated from their average. The 2024 scorecard grades that same average on 2024, and each model's published forecast on its own.

    State-level approval and fundamentals model

    The State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model, by Peter K. Enns, Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = 49.38 + 0.82 × state deviation from the national vote, previous election + 0.33 × state presidential approval (signed toward the Democrat) + 1.43 × weighted change in the state coincident economic index + 2.47 × presidential candidate's home state − 3.49 × presidential candidate's home state, previous election + 1.87 × running mate's home state − 0.35 × Anderson 1980 share (1984 only) − 0.60 × Perot 1996 share (2000 only)

    Fitted on 11 elections × 51 jurisdictions, 1980–2020 (N = 561). Adjusted R² 0.90, standard error 3.46 points.

    The Nowcast runs this regression refitted on 1980–2024 from the authors' replication data, with the 2024 results added, as the authors refit before each forecast. Refitting on 1980–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added: approval 0.30, economy 1.56, home state 2.31, previous home state −3.07, running mate 1.78, standard error 3.45.

    Long-range model

    The Long-Range State-Level Forecast Model, by Jay A. DeSart. It was built to forecast a year ahead, before the nominees are known.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = − 27.423 + 1.017 × the state's Democratic share in the previous election + 0.524 × Democratic share in national head-to-head polls + 2.544 × home state (this election's nominees, minus the previous election's) − 0.988 × consecutive terms the president's party has held the White House (counted as positive for Democrats, negative for Republicans)

    Fitted on seven elections × 50 states, 1996–2020 (N = 350). R² 0.90, standard error 3.08 points.

    The Nowcast runs it refitted on 1996–2024 from DeSart's replication data, with 2024 added. Refitting on 1996–2020 reproduces the numbers above exactly. With 2024 added: previous share 1.007, polls 0.522, home state 1.91, terms −1.00, standard error 2.93.

    How they are combined

    The two models read different things: how people rate the president and how state economies are doing, and what voters say when asked to choose between named candidates. Each state's Nowcast is the average of the two models' forecasts for that state, half each. The Electoral College is then simulated once from those averages, the way the state-level approval and fundamentals model simulates it.

    Equal weights, because the back-tests found no mix that does reliably better: the best one moved around depending on how the test was run. Research on election forecasting has long found that a plain average of forecasts from different methods beats relying on one, and is hard to improve on by fitting weights. Nothing in the combination is fitted to past results: both models run with their authors' equations, and each gets half. The method was fixed on November 12, 2026, and will not be changed on the strength of these same back-tests again, so that 2028 is a fair test of it.

    Why there is no third model

    Several well-known forecasts were tried as a third member of the average, in the same back-tests: counts of Allan Lichtman's Keys to the White House, Helmut Norpoth's Primary Model and Alan Abramowitz's Time for Change model. Each gives one national number, so each was tested the same way: the state map from the first two models, the national level from all three.

    Some third models look a little better than none in the first table: the objective keys and Time for Change all lower the state miss by about 0.2 points and the national miss by 0.1 to 0.2. That is not enough to act on, for three reasons.

    • The whole gain is one election. In 2020 both models overshot the Democrat by about 3 points, and anything that pulled the other way helped. Leave 2020 out (second table) and no third model beats none on the national vote.
    • Choosing by the back-test does not survive a fair test. For each of the five elections, the third model with the best national record in the other four was picked and then scored on the one left out. The pick changed almost every time (Time for Change, six keys, the Primary Model, none, six keys) and the result was a national miss of 1.46 points, worse than the 0.99 from using no third model at all.
    • The longer test does not back a third model either. Forecasting each election from 1996 to 2024 from all the others, adding the keys leaves the national miss unchanged at 1.0 and calls one fewer winner.

    So the Nowcast uses the two published models and nothing else.

    Guarding against fitting the past too closely

    A method can be made to look good on past elections by trying many versions and keeping the best. With five elections to test on, that is a real risk, and an earlier version of this Nowcast ran it: it added a third model, a count of Lichtman's keys, after comparing eight candidates on the same five elections. The check above showed that kind of choosing does not hold up, so it was undone.

    What is left involves no choosing. The two state models are published models run with their authors' equations; nothing in them was tuned here. They are averaged with equal weights, the default when there is no strong reason for anything else. The record quoted on this site is that plain average's record. Each back-test forecast uses only the elections before it. And the method is now fixed: it was frozen on November 12, 2026, before any 2028 result could inform it.

    The evidence is still thinner than it looks. There are 250 state results, but states move together in any one year, so they amount to about five independent tests. A 1.0-point national miss and 96% of states is what the method did on those five; 2020 shows it can miss by 3 points when both models err the same way.

    Primary Model. Norpoth's forecasts as he published them: 50.1% for Obama in 2008, 53.2% for Obama in 2012 and 47.5% for Clinton in 2016, then electoral votes only, 362 for Trump in 2020 and 315 for the Democrat in 2024. The last two were turned into the national vote that would produce them on the previous map. On its own the model missed the national vote by 3.8 points on average and the electoral vote by 64, against 1.0 and 22 for the Nowcast's average. It cannot run until the first primaries in early 2028, so it was left out.

    Time for Change. Abramowitz's model predicts the national vote from the president's June net approval, second-quarter economic growth and whether the president's party is seeking a second term. Refitted from public data (Gallup's June approval and the government's growth figures, as compiled for Harvard's Gov 1347 forecasting course), it reproduces his 2024 forecast to within 0.3 points. It needs a judgment call whenever the economy has a shock like the 2020 lockdown quarter, which is handled here as he handled it, without the growth term. As a third model it lowers the state miss from 2.10 points to 1.89 and the national miss from 0.99 to 0.91 over 2008 to 2024, but does worse than no third model without 2020. It reads mostly the same things as the state-level approval and fundamentals model, and it cannot run until late July 2028, so it was left out.

    The Nowcast page shows each model on its own next to the average, simulated the same way.

    Uncertainty

    As in the authors' code: each forecast draws the coefficients 10,000 times, together, from their covariance; adds model error with a standard deviation equal to the model's root mean square error; and repeats for seven values of the economic weighting, 0.52 to 0.58. That makes 70,000 simulations. The Nowcast uses the same simulation for the averaged forecast.

    The errors of different states are correlated, so when the model misses, it tends to miss the whole map in the same direction. The authors draw that correlation once for each weighting, between 0.70 and 0.97, and so does this site. Rerunning their code on their replication data gives Harris 27 in 100 in 2024, as published, and this site's engine reproduces it.

    The long-range model's own paper gives each state an independent error and shares none. That makes a national miss nearly impossible: its 95% range for the 2024 national vote was 49.4% to 51.9% Democratic, and the result, 49.3%, fell outside it. Its own record shows national misses of 1 to 2 points, which move every state together. So the Nowcast simulates both models with the correlated error above. This site's version of DeSart's equation, run with his independent errors on his 2024 inputs from his replication files, reproduces his published simulation (256 Democratic electoral votes on average, 95% between 218 and 306), which is how it was checked.

    Maine and Nebraska districts take the statewide forecast plus each district's gap from its state in the previous election, as in the authors' code.

    Data behind the Nowcast

    State economies come from the Philadelphia Fed State Coincident Indexes via FRED. Trump's national approval is an average of public polls from VoteHub and PollResults.org. State-by-state approval comes from Civiqs's daily tracker of Trump's job approval (registered voters, all 50 states), as a copy taken on November 10, 2026; only the differences between states are used.

    The 2024 back-test on the scorecard: DeSart's published state forecasts for 2024, from the file dated November 5, 2023 in his replication data (doi:10.7910/DVN/HZGLY9), averaged with the state-level approval and fundamentals forecast published in July 2024.

    Nominees, running mates and their home states are read from Wikipedia each morning. The vice-president adjustment uses job-approval polls of the vice president from VoteHub, or favorability when there are none, as the authors did for 1988. The list of possible candidates follows national 2028 primary polls from PollResults.org.

    Head-to-head polls are national general-election polls between one named Democrat and one named Republican, over the last 180 days. They come from the PollResults.org feed behind its 2028 presidential polls page, which carries the New York Times poll data, and every record in it is read each morning. A poll that tests several pairings counts once for each. Left out: state polls, generic party ballots, questions that name a third candidate, and pairings with anyone not on the candidate list. VoteHub supplies the approval polls; it does not publish general-election pairings yet.

    The 2024 scorecard is the paper's own forecast, recomputed exactly from the authors' replication files. The Nowcast differs from the two papers in the ways listed below.

    Everything that differs from the Enns model

    The 2024 scorecard is the Enns model's own forecast. The 2028 Nowcast runs the same model, refitted with 2024 from the authors' replication files. These are all the places where it does something the authors did not, or fills in something their files do not give. Each is a setting in pipeline/config.json or a line in the site's code.

    Changed or added on purpose

    One more model is averaged in. Each state's forecast is half the Enns model and half DeSart's long-range model, then simulated together. The paper uses its model alone. Why: see "How they are combined" above.

    Each party's field is weighted until its nominee is known. The paper forecasts once the nominees are set, so it has no rule for this. Home-state effects are spread across the possible nominees, an idea taken from DeSart, but by each candidate's share of primary polling. DeSart's own model, as run here, spreads them by how often each candidate appears in the head-to-head polls. The vice-president adjustment is applied in the same proportion: if the sitting vice president has 38% of his party's primary polling, 38% of the adjustment applies.

    The vice-president adjustment is phased in. The authors apply the gap between the vice president's and the president's net approval in full, from a handful of July polls. Here it is scaled by the number of polls of the vice president and reaches full size at 20 polls, so a few polls cannot move every state at once. When there are too few polls in the last 120 days, the window widens to 240 and then 365 days, and as a last resort a single poll is used.

    It runs every day. The paper forecasts once, about 100 days before the election, from approval polls taken in June and July and the economy through June. The Nowcast uses today's polls and the latest economic month, so it describes today, not November 2028. The official forecast on the countdown will follow the paper's timing.

    National vote. The authors add up the national vote by weighting each state by its 2020 population. The Nowcast weights states by the votes cast in 2024.

    From other sources, or inferred

    State approval. The authors estimate approval in each state themselves from national surveys, some of which cannot be shared. The Nowcast uses Civiqs's daily tracker of Trump's job approval instead. Civiqs reports the share of registered voters who approve in each state. Its level runs a few points below the national poll average, so only its differences between states are kept: each state is set to today's national average plus that state's gap from the Civiqs average. The District of Columbia is not in that source and is assumed to sit 24 points below the average state. The figures are a copy taken on November 10, 2026. Civiqs's site does not accept automatic reads, so the copy stays as it is until Civiqs agrees to regular access; differences between states change slowly, so this matters little over a few weeks.

    Florida and the last nominee's home state. The model gives a nominee extra votes in their home state and takes them back at the next election if they are not running again. The authors count Florida as Trump's home state, and he cannot run in 2028, so the model moves Florida toward the Democrat. In the paper's fit that move is 3.49 points, which looked too large, and an earlier version of this site cut it in half by hand. It no longer does. The model is now refitted with the 2024 results, as the authors refit before each forecast, and the 2024 evidence itself lowers the move to 3.07 points; halving it as well would count that evidence twice. California, Harris's home state, moves 3.07 points the other way. The long-range model has its own, smaller version of the same effect: 1.91 points.

    National approval. An average of public polls from VoteHub and PollResults.org over the last 30 days: each pollster's latest poll, weighted by sample size and by age, with recent polls counting more. Internal and partisan polls are left out.

    Approval baseline: 44. The model reads approval minus a baseline. The replication data stores approval with the baseline already taken off, without stating it, but it is 44. The authors' surveys average 44.2% approval, the stored values shift by whole points from one forecast year to the next, and comparing them with national approval in each election from 1980 to 2024 gives 43.6, give or take 0.6. The site used 50 before these files arrived, which overstated the Democrat's share in the state-level approval and fundamentals model by about 2 points.

    Settled by the authors' replication files

    These were guesses until the files arrived. Each now follows the authors' data and code.

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting the authors' data with the 2024 results added. This lowers the fade in the previous nominees' home states from 3.49 to 3.07 points. Until now the site halved the fade, because it looked too large in 2024; the refit now learns that from the 2024 results, so halving it as well would count the same evidence twice. The authors code Trump's 2020 run as Florida; DeSart codes it as New York.

    Economic weighting. 0.55, the median fitted value for 2008 to 2016, simulated from 0.52 to 0.58 in steps of 0.01. The site used 0.40 to 0.70. Economic growth is counted against the Democrat while a Republican is president, as the site assumed.

    Uncertainty. Coefficients are drawn together from their covariance, and the correlation of state errors is drawn between 0.70 and 0.97 for each weighting. The site drew coefficients one at a time and used a fixed split of the error.

    District of Columbia economy and Maine and Nebraska districts. DC uses the average of Maryland and Virginia, and each district follows its state's swing, as the site assumed.

    Where the long-range model differs from DeSart's paper

    Refitted with 2024. The coefficients come from refitting his replication data with 2024 added: his October 2023 poll average (49.53%), the actual nominees' home states and the result. That lowers the home-state effect from 2.54 to 1.91 points.

    Timing. DeSart forecasts once, from polls taken in October of the year before the election. The final forecast does the same: polls conducted in October 2027, fixed on November 7, 2027, with home states spread by poll appearances whoever the nominees turn out to be. Until those polls are fixed, the Nowcast stands in with the last 180 days of polls; from November 7, 2027 it uses the fixed October set, so on August 6, 2028 the Nowcast and the final forecast are the same.

    Error. It is simulated with the correlated error described above, not his independent state errors.

    Which polls. As in the paper, every head-to-head matchup between two named possible nominees counts once in a plain average, and generic party ballots and three-way questions are left out. So are pairings with a two-term president, who cannot be a nominee. Matchups with people outside the site's candidate list count too, and their home states share in the home-state effect. One difference: DeSart took his polls from the RealClearPolitics archive, while the site reads PollResults.org.

    Districts. DeSart awards Maine and Nebraska statewide. Here their districts follow the rule above.
