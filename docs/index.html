About this site

Tipping Point is a personal, non-commercial project. It runs the State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model created by Peter K. Enns (Cornell University and Verasight), Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny, published in PS: Political Science & Politics. The model and its published forecasts are theirs. The 2028 Nowcast also runs a second published model from the same journal, Jay A. DeSart's long-range state-level forecast, which reads head-to-head polls. This site is not affiliated with or endorsed by any of the authors, and any errors in the code are its own.

The idea: presidential elections are largely decided by fundamentals that are knowable months ahead. The authors publish their forecast about 100 days before Election Day. The name comes from the state that decides the Electoral College, and the logo is that idea drawn as districts on a balance beam.

The 2024 scorecard compares the authors' published July 2024 forecast with the certified results, state by state. The forecast numbers are read from the paper's own charts.

The 2028 Nowcast runs that model every morning on the latest Trump approval polls and state economic data, runs DeSart's model on the latest head-to-head polls, and averages the two. You can pick who you think will be on each ticket. It shows what the models say if the election were held today. It is not the forecast, which needs the nominees and June–July 2028 surveys and is due July 30, 2028.

Everything updates automatically: polls, state economies, and the nominees, running mates and home states once the conventions pick them. Data: FRED (Philadelphia Fed state coincident indexes), VoteHub and PollResults.org (poll data, CC BY 4.0), Wikipedia, certified 2024 returns.

    How the models work

    The 2024 scorecard grades one published model. The 2028 Nowcast runs that model and a second one, and averages them. Both predict the Democratic share of the two-party vote in every state, then simulate the Electoral College.

    Approval and economy

    The State Presidential Approval/State Economy Model, by Peter K. Enns, Jonathan Colner, Anusha Kumar and Julius Lagodny.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = 49.38 + 0.82 × state deviation from the national vote, previous election + 0.33 × state presidential approval (signed toward the Democrat) + 1.43 × weighted change in the state coincident economic index + 2.47 × presidential candidate's home state − 3.49 × presidential candidate's home state, previous election + 1.87 × running mate's home state − 0.35 × Anderson 1980 share (1984 only) − 0.60 × Perot 1996 share (2000 only)

    Fitted on 11 elections × 51 jurisdictions, 1980–2020 (N = 561). Adjusted R² 0.90, standard error 3.46 points.

    Head-to-head polls

    The Long-Range State-Level Forecast Model, by Jay A. DeSart. It was built to forecast a year ahead, before the nominees are known.

    Democratic two-party share in a state = − 27.423 + 1.017 × the state's Democratic share in the previous election + 0.524 × Democratic share in national head-to-head polls + 2.544 × home state (this election's nominees, minus the previous election's) − 0.988 × consecutive terms the president's party has held the White House (counted as positive for Democrats, negative for Republicans)

    Fitted on seven elections × 50 states, 1996–2020 (N = 350). R² 0.90, standard error 3.08 points.

    How they are combined

    The Nowcast is the plain average of the two: their odds, their vote shares and their spreads of electoral votes. A state's rating comes from its averaged odds. The models read different things. One reads how people rate the president and how state economies are doing. The other reads what voters say when asked to choose between named candidates. They can disagree widely, and the Nowcast page shows each model's answer next to the average.

    Uncertainty

    Each forecast runs 10,000 draws of the coefficients, adds model error with a standard deviation equal to the model's root mean square error, and repeats across seven values of the economic weighting parameter: 70,000 simulations.

    Most of that error is shared by every state in a simulation, so when the model misses, it tends to miss the whole map in the same direction. Run on the paper's own 2024 state forecasts, this reproduces its published odds: Trump in 73 of 100 simulations.

    The polls model follows its own paper: each state's forecast gets its own random error of 3.08 points, with nothing shared between states, over 20,000 simulations. Because nothing is shared, it is far more certain about the national result, and a one-point move in the polls moves its odds a lot. Run on the 2020 results and the home-state values in DeSart's paper, with the poll average that gives his national forecast of 50.7%, it reproduces his published 2024 simulation: 256 Democratic electoral votes on average, with 95% of simulations between 218 and 306.

    Maine and Nebraska districts take the statewide forecast plus each district's gap from its state in the previous election.

    Ratings

    Solid when a candidate wins more than 98 in 100 simulations, Likely above 75, Lean above 55, Toss-up otherwise.

    The 2024 scorecard's state forecasts and 90% ranges are read from the paper's Figure A-2, which plots each state's simulated distribution; the recovered values reproduce every call and rating in the paper.

    Data behind the Nowcast

    State economies come from the Philadelphia Fed State Coincident Indexes via FRED. Trump's national approval is an average of public polls from VoteHub and PollResults.org. State-level approval estimates come from G. Elliott Morris's Trump Approval Explorer (projects.gelliottmorris.com/trump-approval). Those estimates were last updated on September 22, 2026, so the gaps between states are from then while the national level is current. It will be replaced by Civiqs or Morning Consult estimates if a data-sharing agreement is reached.

    Nominees, running mates and their home states are read from Wikipedia each morning. The vice-president adjustment uses job-approval polls of the vice president from VoteHub, or favorability when there are none, as the authors did for 1988. The list of possible candidates follows national 2028 primary polls from PollResults.org.

    Head-to-head polls are national general-election polls between two people on the candidate list, from PollResults.org, over the last 180 days. A poll that tests several pairings counts once for each.

    The 2024 scorecard uses the paper's own published forecasts, untouched. The Nowcast differs from the two papers in the ways listed below.

    Where the Nowcast differs from the papers

    The coefficients and the simulations are the papers'. These are the places where this site does something they do not, or had to fill in something they do not publish.

    Changes made on purpose

    Two models are averaged. Neither paper does this. The equal weights are this site's choice.

    Each party's field is weighted until its nominee is known. This is DeSart's idea: spread the home-state effect across the possible nominees. In his model the weights are how often each candidate appears in the head-to-head polls, as in his paper. In the approval and economy model, which has no rule for unknown nominees, the weights are shares of primary polling, and the vice-president adjustment is applied by the sitting vice president's share.

    The polls model runs early, on a moving window. DeSart makes one forecast from polls taken in October of the year before the election, which will be October 2027. The Nowcast uses the last 180 days of polls. Pairings that name a third candidate, a generic party label or someone not on the candidate list are left out.

    A chosen nominee is judged on their own polls. If you pick a nominee who has at least five head-to-head polls, the polls model uses those polls, as DeSart did for each pairing in 2016 and 2020. With fewer, it falls back to a wider set and says so.

    Districts in the polls model. DeSart awards Maine and Nebraska statewide. Here their districts are forecast the same way as in the other model.

    The home-state fade is halved, for both parties. The paper gives a nominee about 2.5 points in their home state. Four years later it takes 3.49 points back there, because the last result was inflated by that boost and the next one starts from it. This happens whether or not the person runs again, so it applies to Harris's California and Trump's Florida alike. In 2024 the full fade looked too large everywhere it could be checked. Delaware, where Biden's boost was faded, came in 5.5 points more Democratic than the paper forecast, its largest miss. California moved toward Republicans with Harris on the ballot, so there was little boost to take back. Florida kept moving right between 2020 and 2024 and stayed Republican in the 2026 governor's race without Trump. He had moved his residence there before the 2020 election, though forecasters disagree on whether 2020 counts as a Florida run; DeSart codes it as New York. The approval and economy model applies half the fade, 1.7 points, to both states. The polls model keeps its own fade of 2.5 points in full.

    The vice-president adjustment is phased in. When the sitting vice president is the nominee, the paper replaces the president's net approval with the vice president's, in full. Here the adjustment is scaled by the number of polls of the vice president and reaches full size at 20 polls, so a handful of polls cannot move every state at once.

    It runs every day. The paper makes one forecast about 100 days before the election, with approval from June and July and the economy through June. The Nowcast uses the last 30 days of approval polls and the economy through the latest month, so it describes today, not November 2028. The official forecast on the countdown will follow the paper's timing.

    Gaps filled with assumptions

    All of these are in the approval and economy model, and "the paper" here is Enns and colleagues. DeSart's paper publishes everything his model needs.

    State approval. The paper estimates approval in each state itself, from national surveys. The Nowcast uses G. Elliott Morris's state estimates, shifted so they agree with the national poll average. The District of Columbia is not in that source and is set to 15%.

    Approval baseline. The paper subtracts a fitted constant from approval before it enters the equation and does not report it. The Nowcast uses 50.

    Economic weighting. The paper's weighting parameter for 2024 is not published. The Nowcast uses 0.55, the value in the paper's worked example, and simulates across 0.40 to 0.70. Economic growth is counted against the Democrat while a Republican is president, the same way approval is.

    District of Columbia economy. There is no coincident index for DC, so it uses the average of Maryland and Virginia.

    How errors are shared. The paper does not say how much of its error is common to all states. The split used here, most of it shared, was chosen because it reproduces the paper's published 2024 odds.

    These will be checked against the paper's replication data. Each one is a single setting in pipeline/config.json.
